In Defence of the Album

The Fleeting Lemon

Somewhere along the way, listening to an album became an increasingly optional part of enjoying music. Songs were liberated from their original surroundings, shuffled into playlists and delivered to us by algorithms that seem to know our tastes better than we do.

There's nothing inherently wrong with any of that. Having an almost limitless collection of music available at the touch of a button is extraordinary. But something gets lost when the individual song becomes the only thing that matters.

Because an album is more than a collection of songs. At least, the good ones are.

The lost art of listening

An album asks something of us that modern listening habits don't always encourage: a little patience. It invites us to spend time with an artist's ideas, to follow a sequence of songs and to appreciate how those pieces fit together.

The opening track sets the scene. The quieter moments create space. The unexpected detours keep things interesting. And sometimes a song that seems unremarkable in isolation becomes essential to the record as a whole.

There's a particular satisfaction in reaching the end of an album and feeling that you've experienced something complete.

Not everything needs to be a single

One of the pleasures of listening to full albums is discovering the songs that were never designed to demand immediate attention.

The slow burners. The strange experiments. The instrumental interludes. The tracks that don't reveal themselves until the fifth or sixth listen.

These are often the pieces that give an album its personality. Remove them, and you might still have a handful of excellent songs, but you've lost something of the bigger picture.

Making time for the whole thing

Of course, there's no correct way to listen to music. Playlists are wonderful. Shuffle has its place. Sometimes all you want is one particular song, played very loudly.

But there's still something worth defending about sitting down with a record and letting it unfold as intended. No skipping ahead, no algorithm deciding what comes next, no constant search for something better.

Just an album, from beginning to end.

It might not be the most efficient way to consume music. But then, perhaps music isn't something we should be trying to consume efficiently.
